# Sea research for Whale Club

Research notes for the Whale Club habit game: a night sea with bioluminescence, a night sky and a small shore garden. Each day of showing up unlocks a collectible and shows one short sea fact.

Compiled 2026-10-06 from web sources. Every fact below carries at least one URL that was found during research. Where a popular claim is shaky, it is marked as contested or uncertain rather than dropped. Nothing in this file is personal; it is safe for a public repository.

## 1. Sea facts with sources

### Light in the water

1. **Milky seas.** In 2019 a patch of the Indian Ocean south of Java glowed for weeks over roughly 100,000 square kilometres, about the size of Iceland. The glow comes from luminous bacteria (Vibrio harveyi) that switch on their light only once their population is dense enough, a behaviour called quorum sensing. It was tracked from space by the Suomi NPP satellite.
   - https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hunting-milky-seas-by-satellite-149017/
   - https://eos.org/articles/satellites-allow-scientists-to-dive-into-milky-seas

2. **Dinoflagellate flashes are triggered by shear.** Single-celled dinoflagellates flash when the water around them is sheared by a wave, a fish or a paddle; stretch-sensitive channels in the cell let in calcium, which starts the luciferin reaction. The leading idea for why they do it is the "burglar alarm": a flash reveals the grazer to something bigger that eats the grazer.
   - https://physicsworld.com/a/fluid-flow-and-stress-cause-tiny-marine-organisms-to-emit-light/
   - https://microbewiki.kenyon.edu/index.php/Dinoflagellate_Bioluminescence

3. **The brightest bioluminescent bay.** Mosquito Bay on Vieques, Puerto Rico, holds up to about 700,000 dinoflagellates (Pyrodinium bahamense) per gallon. A narrow mouth keeps them in and mangrove litter feeds them. Guinness listed it as the brightest bioluminescent bay in 2008.
   - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/brightest-bioluminescent-bay

4. **Sea sparkle.** Noctiluca scintillans is a dinoflagellate that colours water rust-red by day in a bloom and burns electric blue at night when disturbed. It does not photosynthesise; it eats other plankton.
   - https://artis.nl/en/artis-micropia/featured-at-artis-micropia/sea-sparkle

5. **Flashlight fish blink to keep formation.** Anomalops katoptron carries a pouch of glowing bacteria under each eye; the bacteria never stop glowing, so the fish "blinks" by rotating or covering the organ. A 2021 study found that higher blink rates pull schoolmates closer together.
   - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7979757/

6. **Firefly squid of Toyama Bay.** Watasenia scintillans is about 7 cm long and carries photophores on its arm tips and around its eyes. Each spring (roughly March to May) a deep canyon funnels millions of them to the surface of Toyama Bay, Japan, which is a designated Special Natural Monument.
   - https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/firefly-squid-toyama-bay

7. **The Atolla alarm.** The deep-sea jellyfish Atolla, when grabbed, runs a pinwheel of blue light around its rim, a "burglar alarm" that calls a bigger predator to attack its attacker. Edith Widder's electronic imitation of this display lured a giant squid to a camera.
   - https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/explorations/15biolum/background/medusa/medusa.html

8. **The largest glowing vertebrate is a shark.** A 2021 study of sharks from the Chatham Rise off New Zealand found that the kitefin shark (Dalatias licha), up to 1.8 m long, glows from its belly and dorsal fins, making it the largest known luminous vertebrate.
   - https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.633582/full
   - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/677825-largest-bioluminescent-vertebrate

9. **A fish with a private red searchlight.** The dragonfish Malacosteus emits far-red light (above 700 nm) that almost no deep-sea animal can see, and it uses a chlorophyll derivative in its own eyes to see it. It hunts by a light its prey cannot detect.
   - https://biolum.eemb.ucsb.edu/organism/dragon.html

10. **Vampire squid.** Vampyroteuthis infernalis is neither a vampire nor a squid but the last member of an ancient cephalopod line. When threatened it releases glowing sticky mucus from its arm tips that can shine for minutes. It lives in oxygen-starved water and eats falling "marine snow" rather than hunting.
    - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/09/120926133239.htm
    - https://aquariumofpacific.org/onlinelearningcenter/species/vampire_squid

11. **Pyrosomes.** A pyrosome is a hollow tube made of hundreds to thousands of tiny cloned animals (tunicates). Some colonies reach several metres. Each zooid glows when it sees light from its neighbours, so a wave of blue-green light runs along the tube. Jellyfish biologist Rebecca Helm called them the "unicorn of the sea".
    - https://www.livingoceansfoundation.org/pyrosomes-fire-salps
    - https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/artful-amoeba/an-earthly-alien-that-s-close-cousin-to-you/

12. **Bloody-belly comb jelly.** This ctenophore lives 250 to 1,500 m down. Red light does not reach the deep, so its crimson stomach is effectively invisible and hides the glow of whatever it has just eaten. The rainbow that runs along its sides is light diffracting on its beating cilia, not bioluminescence.
    - https://www.mbari.org/animal/bloody-belly-comb-jelly

### Deep-sea oddities

13. **Barreleye.** Macropinna microstoma has tubular green eyes inside a transparent, fluid-filled dome on its head; MBARI showed in 2009 that the eyes rotate inside the dome to look up through it or forward. In 5,600 ROV dives MBARI had met the fish only nine times.
    - https://www.mbari.org/news/news_releases/2009/barreleye/barreleye.html

14. **The longest animal ever recorded.** In April 2020 an expedition in the Ningaloo Canyons off Western Australia filmed an Apolemia siphonophore estimated at about 45 metres long, a coiled string of cloned bodies that act as one animal.
    - https://www.bairdmaritime.com/ausmarine/ausmarine-marine-projects/deep-sea-expedition-off-western-australia-reveals-new-marine-animal-species

15. **The Portuguese man o' war is a colony.** It is a siphonophore, not a jellyfish: a set of genetically identical zooids specialised for floating, catching prey, digesting and reproducing, all working as one animal. It has no propulsion and is driven by the wind on its gas-filled sail.
    - https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/portuguese-man-o-war.html

16. **The deepest octopus.** In 2019 a dumbo octopus (Grimpoteuthis) was filmed at 6,957 m in the Java Trench, the first confirmed cephalopod in the hadal zone. The genus is named for the ear-like fins on its head.
    - https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-is-a-dumbo-octopus.html
    - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/83167-deepest-octopus

17. **Anglerfish mates fuse.** In many deep-sea anglerfish the tiny male bites onto the huge female, their tissues fuse and they share a bloodstream. A 2020 study in Science found these species have discarded key parts of the adaptive immune system, which is why the female does not reject him.
    - https://www.livescience.com/anglerfish-fusion-sex-immune-system.html

18. **Yeti crabs farm their food.** Kiwa crabs living at hydrothermal vents grow filamentous bacteria on the hair-like setae of their arms and wave them in the vent flow to feed the crop, then eat it.
    - https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/111202-yeti-crab-bacteria-farming-oceans-science-animals

19. **Hydrothermal vents were found by accident in 1977.** The submersible Alvin, following a warm-water anomaly on the Galapagos Rift, found water at up to 350 C jetting from the seafloor and a crowd of unknown animals (giant tube worms, clams, crabs) living on chemistry rather than sunlight.
    - https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-february-17-1977-hydrothermal-vents-are-discovered

20. **The Lost City.** Found in December 2000 on the Atlantis Massif in the mid-Atlantic, this vent field builds white carbonate chimneys, the tallest about 60 m and still growing, because carbonate is stronger than the sulphide of ordinary black smokers.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_Hydrothermal_Field
    - https://www.sciencenews.org/article/new-type-hydrothermal-vent-looms-large

21. **Whale falls.** A whale carcass on the deep seafloor delivers as much food as thousands of years of marine snow and can support a community for decades. Bone-eating worms (Osedax) have no mouth or gut; bacteria inside them digest the bone, and their microscopic males live inside the females.
    - https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-happens-when-whales-die.html

22. **The twilight zone.** The mesopelagic, roughly 200 to 1,000 m down, may hold more fish by weight than the rest of the ocean combined.
    - https://www.whoi.edu/oceanus/feature/mission-to-the-ocean---s-twilight-zone

23. **The false seafloor.** Second World War sonar operators were puzzled by a "bottom" at 300 to 500 m that rose at night. It was the deep scattering layer: billions of small fish, mostly lanternfish, whose swim bladders reflect sound.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_scattering_layer

24. **The largest migration on Earth happens every night.** An estimated ten billion tonnes of zooplankton, small fish and jellies rise toward the surface at dusk to feed and sink again at dawn to hide. It happens in every ocean, every day.
    - https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/greatest-migration-on-earth-happens-under-darkness-every-day
    - https://kids.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2020.00044

25. **Lanternfish.** Myctophids may account for up to 65 percent of all deep-sea fish biomass. Hardly anyone has seen one alive.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanternfish

26. **Challenger Deep.** The deepest known point of the ocean, in the Mariana Trench, was remeasured in 2021 at about 10,935 m. Until 2019 only three people had been to the bottom (Piccard and Walsh in 1960, James Cameron in 2012). The old line "fewer people than have walked on the moon" is now out of date: repeated dives since 2019 have carried more people down.
    - https://kesq.com/news/national-world/cnn-world/2023/06/23/6-incredible-facts-about-the-challenger-deep-the-deepest-point-on-earth/

27. **Most of the seafloor is still unmapped in detail.** The Seabed 2030 project reported in June 2025 that 27.3 percent of the ocean floor had been mapped to modern standards.
    - https://tnfsa.nippon-foundation.or.jp/news/2756701
    - https://www.maproomblog.com/2025/06/27-3-of-the-ocean-floor-has-now-been-mapped/

28. **The largest waterfall is underwater.** The Denmark Strait cataract, between Iceland and Greenland, is a flow of cold dense water dropping about 3,500 m down a slope, several times the height of any waterfall on land. Nothing is visible at the surface.
    - https://livescience.com/planet-earth/rivers-oceans/denmark-strait-cataract-the-worlds-largest-waterfall-hidden-underwater-and-unlike-any-other-on-land

29. **Brinicles.** Under Antarctic sea ice, very cold brine sinks from the ice in a plume and freezes the seawater around it into a hollow finger of ice. Filmed in time-lapse for the BBC series Frozen Planet in 2011, one reached the seabed and froze starfish and urchins in its path.
    - https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/all-the-conditions-required-for-life-to-appear-are-here-in-antarcticas-amazing-ice-stalactites-18088357/

30. **Giant larvaceans build a new house every day.** Bathochordaeus, a tadpole-shaped animal a few centimetres long, spins a mucus filter house up to a metre wide, then abandons it daily; the sinking houses carry a large share of carbon to the seafloor.
    - https://www.mbari.org/news/midwater-ecology-expedition-2018-log-4/
    - https://www.sciencenews.org/article/sea-creatures-sticky-mucus-houses-catch-ocean-carbon-really-fast

31. **Glass squid.** Cranchiid squid are almost fully transparent, fill a body chamber with ammonia solution lighter than seawater to float without effort, and carry photophores under their eyes to erase the eyes' shadows.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranchiidae

32. **Sea pigs.** Scotoplanes are pink sea cucumbers that walk the abyssal plain on inflatable tube feet. MBARI found juvenile king crabs hitching rides on and hiding under them.
    - https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/guest-blog/why-are-juvenile-crabs-hitching-rides-on-sea-pigs/

33. **The giant isopod that would not eat.** A 30 cm Bathynomus at Toba Aquarium, Japan, last ate in January 2009 and was found dead in February 2014, five years and 43 days later.
    - https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/fishkeeping-news/giant-isopod-dies-after-five-year-hunger-strike/

34. **The longest brooding known in any animal.** MBARI watched a female deep-sea octopus (Graneledone boreopacifica) guard one clutch of eggs on a canyon wall 1,400 m down for 53 months, from 2007 to 2011, apparently without eating.
    - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4116195/

35. **First live colossal squid.** On 9 March 2025, 100 years after the species was described, an ROV filmed a 30 cm juvenile colossal squid at 600 m near the South Sandwich Islands. Every previous specimen had been dead.
    - https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/colossal-squid-sighting-deep-sea

36. **Sea spiders keep their guts in their legs.** Pycnogonids have no gills and almost no body; the digestive tract runs into the legs, oxygen diffuses through the cuticle, and waves of gut contraction pump the blood.
    - https://www.sciencenews.org/article/it-takes-guts-sea-spider-pump-blood

37. **Fish with clear blood.** Antarctic icefish are the only vertebrates without haemoglobin. Their blood is translucent, their hearts white, and they survive because very cold water holds enough dissolved oxygen.
    - https://www.sciencefriday.com/segments/this-antarctic-fish-has-blood-as-white-as-snow/

38. **The Titanic is being eaten.** Rusticles hanging from the wreck are built by bacterial communities including Halomonas titanicae, a species named for the ship in 2010, which digests the iron.
    - https://www.discovermagazine.com/new-bacterium-is-digesting-the-titanic-one-iron-atom-at-a-time-2817

39. **Hagfish slime.** A hagfish releases a small amount of mucus and coiled protein threads that unravel in seawater and expand to about 10,000 times their volume in under half a second, clogging a predator's gills.
    - https://www.popsci.com/hagfish-slime-expands/

40. **Oarfish.** Regalecus glesne is the longest bony fish, reliably to about 8 m (claims to 11 m exist). It is called "king of herrings" and in Japan a harbinger of earthquakes; there is no evidence for the earthquake link.
    - https://livescience.com/animals/fish/giant-oarfish-the-doomsday-fish-of-legend-that-supposedly-foreshadows-earthquakes

41. **Bobbit worm.** Eunice aphroditois lives buried in tropical sand with only its antennae showing and can reach about 3 m. Its jaws snap shut fast enough to cut a fish in half. Some reef fish answer by blowing jets of water at it until it retreats.
    - https://livescience.com/animals/sand-striker-the-glittering-bobbit-worm-that-chops-fish-in-half-with-its-retractable-jaws
    - https://unibas.ch/en/News-Events/News/Uni-Research/Fish-Against-Monster-Worms.html

### Whales, dolphins and big fish

42. **The 52-hertz whale.** Since 1989 a whale calling at 52 Hz, far above the 15 to 20 Hz of blue and fin whales, has been tracked in the North Pacific on US Navy hydrophones, first noticed by William Watkins at Woods Hole. Its species is unknown; a hybrid or an unusual individual are the usual guesses. The "loneliest whale" story is a human reading: no one knows whether other whales hear it or ignore it. (Contested.)
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/52-hertz_whale

43. **Narwhal tusks are sense organs.** The tusk is a spiral tooth with about ten million nerve endings open to the water; it can register temperature, pressure and salinity changes.
    - https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2005/12/marine-biology-mystery-solved/
    - https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/narwhal-tusks-are-actually-nerve-rich-teeth

44. **Sperm whales sleep standing up.** Tags on wild sperm whales (Miller and colleagues, 2008) recorded "drift dives": groups hanging motionless and silent, nose up, just under the surface for 10 to 15 minutes at a time.
    - https://news.st-andrews.ac.uk/archive/drifting-off-to-sleep/

45. **The loudest animal.** Sperm whale echolocation clicks have been measured at over 230 decibels, the loudest sound made by any animal.
    - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/70669-loudest-animal-sound

46. **Sperm whale clans have dialects.** Sperm whales live in matrilineal clans that share patterns of clicks called codas; the patterns are learned, not inherited, and clans with different dialects share the same waters without mixing. Hal Whitehead's work on this began around the Galapagos.
    - https://dal.ca/news/2015/09/08/cracking-the--coda---what-pacific-sperm-whales-tell-us-about-cul.html

47. **Humpback song revolutions.** Male humpbacks share one song per population that slowly evolves. In the South Pacific, whole new songs sweep eastward from eastern Australia through French Polynesia to Ecuador and replace the old one, a cultural transmission across an ocean.
    - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9428538/

48. **Bubble nets are tools.** A 2024 study in Royal Society Open Science showed solitary humpbacks in Alaska adjusting the number of rings, size, depth and bubble spacing of their nets, catching up to seven times more krill per lunge; the authors call it tool use.
    - https://www.npr.org/2024/09/06/nx-s1-5087900/humpback-whales-krill-bubble-net-feeding

49. **A blue whale's heart can slow to two beats a minute.** The first ECG of a wild blue whale (Stanford, 2019) recorded two beats per minute at depth and about 37 at the surface.
    - https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2019/11/first-ever-recording-blue-whales-heart-rate

50. **Bowhead whales live past 200.** Eye-lens chemistry put one Alaskan bowhead at about 211 years, and hunters have found stone and ivory harpoon tips, unused since the 1880s, embedded in living whales.
    - https://www.sciencenews.org/article/cetacean-seniors
    - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/461724-longest-lived-whale

51. **Deepest and longest dive by a mammal.** Cuvier's beaked whales have been tracked to 2,992 m and on one dive stayed down 222 minutes.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuvier%27s_beaked_whale
    - https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/secrets-of-deep-diving-whales.html

52. **Whale earwax is a diary.** Baleen whales build an earplug of wax in layers, roughly one per six months, that records hormones such as cortisol and pollutants such as DDT and mercury across the whole life of the whale.
    - https://www.npr.org/2013/09/17/223139796/ear-wax-from-whales-keeps-record-of-ocean-contaminants

53. **Dolphins sleep one half of the brain at a time.** The other half stays awake to surface and breathe; the eye on the awake side stays open.
    - https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/meet-the-animals-that-literally-sleep-with-one-eye-open/

54. **Whale sharks are catalogued like star fields.** Each whale shark's spot pattern is unique. Researchers adapted the Groth algorithm, written in 1986 to match star patterns in telescope images, to match photos of whale sharks; the Wildbook catalogue holds tens of thousands of sightings.
    - https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/whale-shark-identification-catalog-telescope-algorithm
    - https://petapixel.com/2022/06/10/an-algorithm-for-mapping-stars-is-also-used-to-track-whale-sharks/

### Long lives

55. **Greenland shark.** Radiocarbon dating of eye-lens cores (Nielsen and colleagues, Science, 2016) gave the largest shark an age of about 392 years, with a possible range of 272 to 512, making it the longest-lived vertebrate known. The exact number is uncertain.
    - https://news.mongabay.com/2016/08/400-year-old-greenland-shark-is-the-longest-living-vertebrate/

56. **A sponge 11,000 years old.** Monorhaphis chuni, a deep Pacific glass sponge, anchors itself with a single silica spicule up to 3 m long that grows in annual layers; one specimen is estimated at about 11,000 years and records deep-sea temperature changes.
    - https://www.mpg.de/5595233/climate_archive_deep-sea_sponge

57. **Ming the clam.** An ocean quahog dredged off Iceland in 2006 was aged from its shell rings at 507 years, born around 1499. It was killed when it was opened for study.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_(clam)
    - https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/86633-longest-lived-animal

58. **The immortal jellyfish.** Turritopsis dohrnii, about 4.5 mm wide, can respond to injury or starvation by reverting its adult cells to a polyp and growing up again, in principle indefinitely. In the wild most still get eaten.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turritopsis_dohrnii

### Invertebrate oddities and shore life

59. **Mantis shrimp.** Their eyes carry 12 to 16 kinds of photoreceptor (humans have three). Smashing species strike at about 23 m/s with accelerations around 10,000 g, and the collapsing cavitation bubble hits the prey a second time.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantis_shrimp

60. **Pistol shrimp.** The snap of its claw creates a cavitation bubble that collapses with a sound around 210 dB and, for an instant, a temperature estimated near 4,700 C, with a faint flash of light. (Temperature is an estimate from the sonoluminescence literature.)
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpheidae

61. **Octopuses change colour in their sleep.** A 2023 study in Nature on Octopus laqueus found a quiet, pale sleep broken about once an hour by a minute of active sleep in which the skin cycles through waking patterns and brain activity resembles wakefulness, suggesting something like dreaming.
    - https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/06/evidence-octopuses-complex-sleep
    - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10322707

62. **Octopus with a coconut.** Veined octopuses in Indonesia carry halves of coconut shell under their bodies with an awkward stilt-walk, then assemble them into a shelter later; a 2009 Current Biology paper called it the first tool use in an invertebrate.
    - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/12/091214121953.htm

63. **Three hearts, blue blood.** An octopus has two gill hearts and one body heart, copper-based blue blood, and the body heart stops beating while it swims, which is why octopuses prefer to crawl.
    - https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-does-an-octopus-have-more-than-one-heart

64. **Blanket octopus.** The female can reach 2 m and the male about 2.4 cm, the largest size gap between sexes in the animal kingdom. Small individuals tear stinging tentacles off Portuguese man o' war and hold them as weapons.
    - https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/blanket-octopus

65. **Sea slugs that steal weapons and solar panels.** Aeolid nudibranchs eat anemones and hydroids and store the unfired stinging cells in sacs at the tips of their back tufts (cerata) for their own defence. Some sacoglossan slugs keep chloroplasts from their algae alive inside them, though how much energy they get from photosynthesis is debated.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudibranch
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptoplasty

66. **Comet stars.** Sea stars of the genus Linckia can regrow an entire animal from one arm; the regrowing stub looks like a comet with a tail. Most other sea stars need part of the central disc.
    - https://livingoceansfoundation.org/sea-stars-starfish-of-great-barrier-reef

67. **Sea cucumber evisceration.** When attacked, some sea cucumbers expel sticky Cuvierian tubules or even parts of their gut and respiratory trees through the body wall, then regrow them over weeks.
    - https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/nature/sea-cucumbers.html

68. **Sea angels and sea butterflies.** Clione limacina is a 3 to 5 cm shell-less Arctic snail that swims with two wing-like flaps of its foot and eats only shelled sea butterflies, hooking them out of their shells. Its own larva has a shell, which it loses as it grows.
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clione_limacina

69. **Moon jellyfish.** Aurelia aurita is about 95 percent water, has no brain or heart, and is identified by the four horseshoe-shaped gonads visible through its bell.
    - https://www.marlin.ac.uk/species/detail/2089

70. **Pufferfish sand circles.** For years divers off Amami Oshima, Japan, found 2 m geometric circles in the sand with no explanation. In 2011 the maker was found: a 12 cm male white-spotted pufferfish that spends about a week carving each one as a nest to attract a female.
    - https://resou.osaka-u.ac.jp/en/research/2018/20180830_1

71. **Coral mass spawning.** On the Great Barrier Reef, corals release eggs and sperm together on a few nights after the full moons of October to December, when the water is around 27 to 28 C. The popular claim that the slick is visible from space is often repeated but weakly sourced. (Contested detail.)
    - https://queensland.com/places-to-see/experiences/great-barrier-reef/coral-spawning.html

72. **Sea otters and kelp.** Otters rest in groups called rafts and wrap themselves in kelp so they do not drift away while sleeping. Holding paws has been documented but is rare in the wild; the famous images are mostly from aquaria. (Hand-holding as a routine habit is contested.)
    - https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/sea-otters-hold-hands-while-sleeping-and-they-even-cuddle

## 3. Collectible ideas, common to rare

Chosen for how well they read as a small drawing in a dark scene with a glowing sea. Each entry: name, what it looks like, why it is interesting.

1. **Sea sparkle.** Blue flecks along the edge of a wave or a footprint on wet sand. The first and most common glow; it only appears when something moves. (4, 2)
2. **Moon jellyfish.** A pale translucent disc with four faint horseshoe rings. 95 percent water, no brain, drifting past at night. (69)
3. **Sea butterfly.** A tiny snail with two clear wings, smaller than a fingernail. A swimming snail; food for the sea angel. (68)
4. **Lanternfish.** A small silver fish with a row of blue dots along its belly. Probably the most numerous fish on earth and almost never seen. (25)
5. **Flashlight fish.** A dark fish shape with two blinking cheek lights. It cannot turn the light off, only hide it. (5)
6. **Comb jelly.** A clear oval with a rainbow running down its ribs. Not glow but diffraction from beating hairs. (12)
7. **Comet star.** A sea star with one long arm and four short stubs. Regrowing a whole body from one arm. (66)
8. **Nudibranch.** A bright slug with tufted back. Stores stolen stings in the tips of the tufts. (65)
9. **Pufferfish circle.** A geometric ring in the sand, seen from above. A week of work by a 12 cm fish. (70)
10. **Firefly squid.** A small squid with glowing arm tips. Spring in Toyama Bay. (6)
11. **Sea otter in kelp.** An otter on its back, wrapped in a kelp strand, under stars. Anchored so it does not drift. (72)
12. **Pyrosome.** A long faint tube with light travelling along it. Thousands of animals in one body. (11)
13. **Portuguese man o' war.** A blue-violet sail at the surface with long trailing threads. A colony, not an animal. (15)
14. **Atolla jellyfish.** A deep red crown jelly with a pinwheel of blue light. A burglar alarm in the dark. (7)
15. **Dolphin.** A dolphin at the surface with one eye open. Sleeping half a brain at a time. (53)
16. **Barreleye.** A dark fish with a glass dome and two green eyes inside it. Looks up through its own head. (13)
17. **Glass squid.** An almost invisible outline with two eye lights. Floats on ammonia. (31)
18. **Vampire squid.** A cloaked shape with glowing arm tips. Neither vampire nor squid. (10)
19. **Dumbo octopus.** A round octopus with two ear-fins. Filmed nearly 7 km down. (16)
20. **Humpback bubble net.** A spiral ring of bubbles seen from above, whale rising through the middle. A net made of breath. (48)
21. **Narwhal.** A pale whale with a spiral tusk under the moon. The tusk is a sense organ. (43)
22. **Brinicle.** A white finger of ice reaching down from the ice ceiling to the seabed. Freezes what it touches. (29)
23. **Sunken ship.** A wreck with orange rusticles hanging like icicles. Being eaten by bacteria. (38)
24. **Whale fall.** A whale skeleton on the floor with red bone-worm fuzz and small pale animals. A decades-long feast. (21)
25. **The deep opens.** A vent chimney in the black, either a black smoker with red-tipped tube worms or a white Lost City tower. Life without sunlight, found in 1977. (19, 20)

Extras if the list grows: milky sea (a horizon-wide pale glow under a moonless sky, 1), sea angel (68), colossal squid juvenile (35), 45 m siphonophore (14), sleeping sperm whales standing in a group (44), 52-hertz whale as a sound-only collectible (42), glass sponge with its 3 m needle (56), sea pig with crab riders (32).

## 4. Underrated books and sources about the sea

Titles and authors were checked against publisher, library or review pages during research unless marked otherwise.

### Nonfiction

- **The Sea Around Us** (Rachel Carson, 1951). The book that made oceanography lyrical; still the best short introduction to how the ocean works and feels.
- **The Edge of the Sea** (Rachel Carson, 1955). Carson on the shoreline, tide pools and the animals of the margin; the quieter sibling of The Sea Around Us. (Title from memory, widely known; not re-verified in this session.)
- **Half Mile Down** (William Beebe, 1934). Beebe's own account of the bathysphere dives off Bermuda, the first human eyes on living deep-sea bioluminescence.
- **The Bathysphere Book: Effects of the Luminous Ocean Depths** (Brad Fox, 2023). A collage-style retelling of the Beebe and Barton dives built from logs and vignettes; a Washington Post top ten book of 2023.
- **The Outermost House** (Henry Beston, 1928). A year alone in a cottage on the Cape Cod dunes; tides, storms, birds and night on the beach.
- **The Log from the Sea of Cortez** (John Steinbeck, 1951). Steinbeck and biologist Ed Ricketts collecting in the Gulf of California; part tide-pool science, part philosophy. (Title from memory; not re-verified in this session.)
- **Below the Edge of Darkness: A Memoir of Exploring Light and Life in the Deep Sea** (Edith Widder, 2021). The bioluminescence scientist who lured the first giant squid to a camera.
- **The Brilliant Abyss** (Helen Scales, 2021). The deep sea as a living system and what mining and fishing threaten.
- **Eye of the Shoal** (Helen Scales, 2018). A natural history of fish: colour, sound, deception and night dives with glowing fish.
- **Spying on Whales** (Nick Pyenson, 2018). Whale evolution, present and future, by a Smithsonian palaeontologist.
- **Fathoms: The World in the Whale** (Rebecca Giggs, 2020). Whales as the place where ecology, culture and pollution meet; began with a stranded humpback in Australia.
- **Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness** (Peter Godfrey-Smith, 2016). A philosopher-diver on cephalopod minds.
- **The Soul of an Octopus** (Sy Montgomery, 2015). Friendships with named octopuses at the New England Aquarium. (Title from memory; widely known; not re-verified in this session.)
- **Why Fish Don't Exist** (Lulu Miller, 2020). A biography of taxonomist David Starr Jordan that turns into something stranger and darker.
- **What a Fish Knows: The Inner Lives of Our Underwater Cousins** (Jonathan Balcombe, 2016). Fish cognition, play, tool use and cooperation.
- **Spineless: The Science of Jellyfish and the Art of Growing a Backbone** (Juli Berwald, 2017). Jellyfish biology woven with a memoir of returning to science.
- **Kraken: The Curious, Exciting, and Slightly Disturbing Science of Squid** (Wendy Williams, 2011). Squid, octopus and cuttlefish science told with humour.
- **The Deep: The Extraordinary Creatures of the Abyss** (Claire Nouvian, 2007). A large-format photo book of deep-sea animals with essays by researchers; the best visual reference for drawing the collectibles above.
- **Mapping the Deep: The Extraordinary Story of Ocean Science** (Robert Kunzig, 2000). A history of oceanography and the people who built it; published in the US as The Restless Sea.
- **The Unnatural History of the Sea** (Callum Roberts, 2007). What the ocean was like before industrial fishing, reconstructed from logbooks. (Title from memory; not re-verified in this session.)
- **The Outlaw Ocean** (Ian Urbina, 2019). Crime, slavery and lawlessness on the high seas, from a Pulitzer-winning reporter.
- **Deep: Freediving, Renegade Science, and What the Ocean Tells Us About Ourselves** (James Nestor, 2014). Freedivers, the mammalian dive reflex and the fringes of ocean science.
- **The Blue Machine: How the Ocean Works** (Helen Czerski, 2023). The ocean as a single engine powered by sunlight; won the 2024 Wainwright Prize for Conservation.
- **The Underworld: Journeys to the Depths of the Ocean** (Susan Casey, 2023). Dives to the deepest places with the people who go there.
- **Sing Like Fish: How Sound Rules Life Under Water** (Amorina Kingdon, 2024). Underwater sound, from midshipman drumming to whale song syntax.

### Fiction

- **The Kraken Wakes** (John Wyndham, 1953). Something arrives in the deep ocean and the sea is turned against humanity; quiet British apocalypse.
- **The Mountain in the Sea** (Ray Nayler, 2022). A hyper-intelligent octopus species off Vietnam; won the 2023 Locus Award for best first novel.
- **Our Wives Under the Sea** (Julia Armfield, 2022). A wife returns changed from months trapped in a deep-sea vessel; grief as gothic horror.
- **Whalefall** (Daniel Kraus, 2023). A diver swallowed by a sperm whale has one hour of air; scientifically grounded thriller. Note the one-word title.
- **Remarkably Bright Creatures** (Shelby Van Pelt, 2022). A night cleaner at a small aquarium and a giant Pacific octopus who is smarter than anyone suspects.
- **Moby-Dick** (Herman Melville, 1851) and **The Old Man and the Sea** (Ernest Hemingway, 1952). Not underrated, but the two the others are answering.

- Items marked contested: the "lonely" reading of the 52-hertz whale (42), coral spawning "visible from space" (71), sea otters holding hands as a habit (72), the benefit of chloroplasts to "solar-powered" slugs (65), the exact Greenland shark age (55, wide error bars), the pistol shrimp bubble temperature (60, an estimate), and the "fewer people than the moon" line about Challenger Deep (26, now out of date).
- The mantis shrimp and pistol shrimp figures (59, 60) are the standard numbers given on Wikipedia and in popular science; the primary measurements are older papers that were not pulled in this session.
- Four book entries are marked "title from memory" (The Edge of the Sea, The Log from the Sea of Cortez, The Soul of an Octopus, The Unnatural History of the Sea). They are well-known titles and the attribution is very likely correct, but they were not re-checked against a publisher page in this session.
